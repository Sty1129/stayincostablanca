A property in Costa Blanca that remains unoccupied for several weeks or months can develop problems that are not immediately obvious.

A small water leak, increasing humidity, a drainage issue or a malfunctioning air-conditioning system can remain unnoticed until the owner returns. By then, a relatively minor issue may have developed into a more expensive repair.

This is why preventive property management is not simply about checking whether everything “looks fine”. It is about creating a regular, documented process that makes changes easier to identify over time.

## 1. Check access points and security

The first step during a property visit should always be a visual inspection of the main access points.

This includes checking:

- entrance doors;
- locks;
- windows;
- shutters;
- gates;
- terraces and other accessible exterior areas;
- visible signs of forced entry or damage.

The objective is not only to confirm that the property is secure at that particular moment, but also to identify anything that has changed since the previous visit.

For a property owner living in another country, regular documented checks provide a much clearer picture of what is happening at the property while they are away.

## 2. Check water and sanitary installations

Water-related problems can cause significant damage when a property remains unattended.

A preventive visit should include basic checks of areas such as:

- taps;
- toilets;
- visible pipe connections;
- sinks;
- showers;
- areas around sanitary installations;
- visible signs of leakage or unexpected moisture.

Where appropriate, photographing the water meter can also help create a simple consumption history.

An unexpected change between readings does not automatically mean there is a leak, but it can provide a useful reason to investigate further.

Regular checks can therefore help identify unusual situations before they develop into larger problems.

## 3. Monitor humidity and the indoor environment

Humidity is particularly relevant in coastal properties.

A home can appear perfectly normal while environmental conditions are gradually changing.

Visual inspection should therefore look for signs such as:

- condensation;
- damp patches;
- mould;
- unusual odours;
- changes around windows or external walls;
- signs of water infiltration.

In more detailed monitoring, temperature and relative humidity readings can also provide useful context.

The important point is not a single isolated measurement.

What becomes particularly useful is the ability to compare readings and observations from one visit to another.

A change over time can provide an early indication that something deserves closer attention.

## 4. Ventilate the property when appropriate

A closed property can accumulate humidity, stale air and odours, particularly when it remains unused for long periods.

Periodic ventilation can therefore form part of a preventive property-care routine.

Depending on the property and the service agreed with the owner, this may include natural ventilation or assisted ventilation.

The purpose is not to claim that occasional ventilation completely replaces the indoor air or eliminates every humidity-related risk.

Instead, it is one practical measure that can contribute to maintaining better indoor conditions when combined with regular monitoring.

## 5. Check terraces, drainage and exterior areas

Exterior areas deserve particular attention in Costa Blanca because strong rain, wind and prolonged exposure to the sun can affect a property even when nobody is living there.

Useful checks may include:

- terrace drainage;
- visible water accumulation;
- awnings and pergolas;
- outdoor furniture;
- railings;
- visible exterior damage;
- signs of infiltration;
- objects that may have moved during strong winds.

Blocked drainage can appear insignificant during dry weather but become much more important during heavy rainfall.

Identifying the problem beforehand may prevent unnecessary water accumulation or damage.

## 6. Carry out basic equipment checks

Equipment that remains unused for extended periods can also develop problems.

Depending on the agreed inspection scope, a visit may include basic functional checks of:

- air conditioning;
- electrical supply;
- lighting;
- television or satellite systems;
- other installed equipment relevant to the property.

These are not technical maintenance inspections.

The objective is to identify obvious changes in operation that may justify further investigation by an appropriate technician.

A property-management visit should detect and document potential issues, not replace qualified technical servicing.

## 7. Photograph utility meters

Photographing utility meters during scheduled visits creates a simple but useful record.

Over time, these photographs can help owners compare:

- water readings;
- electricity readings;
- changes between visits;
- unexpected variations in consumption.

A single photograph provides limited information.

A series of dated photographs provides context.

That distinction is important.

Preventive property management becomes much more useful when observations can be compared instead of relying only on memory.

## 8. Document every visit

One of the most important parts of professional property management is documentation.

A useful property report should provide the owner with a clear record of what was checked and what was observed.

Depending on the service level, this can include:

- photographs;
- meter readings;
- temperature and humidity values;
- observations;
- identified changes;
- incidents;
- recommendations;
- actions requiring further attention.

The purpose is to create continuity between visits.

Instead of receiving only a message saying that “everything is fine”, the owner has a documented history of the property.

## Why preventive property care matters

Owners who live abroad often rely on neighbours, friends or occasional visits to check their property.

That can work for simple situations, but it usually does not create a consistent inspection history.

Professional key holding and property management introduce structure into the process.

The objective is not to promise that every possible incident can be prevented.

No property-management service can guarantee that.

The objective is to reduce the period during which a problem may remain unnoticed and to provide the owner with clear, documented information about the condition of the property.

For a home that remains unoccupied for significant periods, that difference can be very valuable.

## Conclusion

Protecting an unoccupied property in Costa Blanca is not about carrying out one large inspection once a year.

It is about consistency.

Regular access and security checks, water monitoring, humidity observation, ventilation, exterior checks and documented reports can help identify changes before they become more serious.

For owners who do not live permanently in Spain, a structured preventive approach provides something particularly important: visibility over what is happening at their property while they are away.
